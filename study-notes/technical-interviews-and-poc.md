# Technical Interviews & Proof of Concepts (explained simply)

## What is a Proof of Concept (POC)?

A **Proof of Concept** is a small, minimal, working demonstration built to confirm that a core idea or technical approach actually works — **before** investing lots of time polishing, optimizing, or fully building it out.

**Why this matters in interviews specifically:** when given a complex coding challenge or system design question, it's tempting to want to jump straight to a perfect, fully-optimized solution. But the better approach is to first get *something* minimally working that proves your core idea is sound, and only refine/optimize it afterward if time allows. This avoids wasting time perfecting an approach that might turn out to be fundamentally flawed.

**Simple analogy:** think of it like sketching a rough draft of an essay before carefully editing sentence-by-sentence — you want to make sure the overall structure/argument actually works first, before polishing individual wording, since polishing an outline you'll end up scrapping wastes effort.

## How this connects to your own coursework

This is actually the same instinct behind how we've been building your class projects step by step — get the triangle/square moving and rendering correctly *first* (the core proof that the approach works), and only *then* refine details like stroke thickness, fill color, or size. Jumping straight to fine-tuning visual details before confirming the basic rendering logic works would be backwards.

## Handling ambiguous or difficult interview questions

Rather than guessing blindly or freezing up when a question feels unclear, the recommended approach is a structured sequence:

1. **Ask clarifying questions** — narrow down exactly what's being asked, rather than assuming. Real-world problems (and interview questions modeling them) are often intentionally left a bit vague to see if you'll ask the right questions.
2. **State your working assumptions out loud** — if you do have to make an assumption to move forward, say it explicitly, so the interviewer knows exactly what you're basing your approach on (and can correct you if that assumption is wrong).
3. **Fall back on strong foundational principles** — when unsure of the "clever" or optimal solution, lean on solid, reliable fundamentals you actually understand well, rather than guessing at something more advanced you're unsure about.
4. **Narrate your reasoning step by step** — walk the interviewer through *how* you're thinking, not just your final answer. This lets them see your problem-solving process, which is often what's actually being evaluated — not just whether you land on the exact right answer.

## Why communication matters as much as the technical answer

The lecture frames interviews as being evaluated on **problem-solving methodology, architectural clarity, and communication under pressure** — not purely on whether you produce a perfect answer instantly. This reframes the goal: an interviewer generally wants to see *how you think* and *how you'd behave on a real team* when facing something unclear, since that's a more realistic predictor of how you'd actually perform on the job than memorized answers to predictable questions.

## Key takeaway
> Build a minimal working Proof of Concept before optimizing. When facing an ambiguous question, ask clarifying questions, state your assumptions, rely on strong fundamentals, and narrate your reasoning clearly — the process matters as much as the final answer.
